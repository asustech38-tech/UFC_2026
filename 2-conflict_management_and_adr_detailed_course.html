Chapter 1

Conflict and Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)

Understanding the fundamental nature, levels, and evolution of conflict management, and framing ADR within organizational and legal structures.

1.2.1 Definition & Levels of Conflict

Definition: A process that begins when one party perceives that another party has negatively affected, or is about to negatively affect, something the first party cares about. It is a clash of interests, goals, values, or resources.

Intrapersonal

Occurs within an individual due to competing personal goals, role ambiguity, or conflicting ethical values.

Interpersonal

Arises between two or more individuals due to personality clashes, competition for resources, or poor communication.

Intergroup

Occurs between teams, departments, or divisions (e.g., marketing vs. production over budget allocation).

Interorganizational

Arises between external companies (e.g., market competition or contractual disputes).

1.2.2 Theoretical Perspectives

1. Traditional View

Assumed all conflict is inherently bad, harmful, and must be avoided/eliminated immediately. Stems from poor communication.

2. Human Relations View

Positioned conflict as a natural and inevitable outcome in any group. Cannot be eliminated; should be accepted and managed.

3. Interactionist View

Proposes conflict is not only natural but necessary for innovation. A completely harmonious organization risks becoming static.

1.3.1 Sources of Conflict

Resource Scarcity: Budgets, staffing, space.
Task Interdependence: Success relies on another's output.
Role Ambiguity: Unclear boundaries/expectations.
Goal Incompatibility: Misaligned department objectives.
1.3.2 Impact: Functional Conflict improves decisions and stimulates innovation. Dysfunctional Conflict hinders performance and creates toxicity.

1.4 The Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) Continuum

ADR encompasses practices to resolve disputes outside the formal judicial court system, aiming for satisfactory, timely, and cost-effective outcomes.

Low Binding / High Control

1. Negotiation

Direct interactive process. Parties retain full control over process & outcome.

Spacer

2. Mediation

Neutral 3rd party facilitates dialogue. No authority to impose binding decisions.

Spacer

3. Conciliation

Active 3rd party makes formal, non-binding recommendations based on analysis.

High Binding / Low Control

4. Arbitration

Quasi-judicial. Neutral 3rd party holds authority to issue final, binding Arbitral Award.

...leading ultimately to Traditional Litigation (Court).

1.6 Integrated Conflict Mgmt Systems (ICMS)

Modern organizations adopt ICMS to resolve disputes internally before escalation:

  • Open-Door Policies: Direct access to upper-management.
  • Organizational Ombudsperson: Neutral internal entity for confidential guidance.
  • Peer Review Panels: Peer employees assessing workplace grievances.
  • Step-rate Dispute Clauses: Contractual mandates (Negotiation → Mediation → Arbitration).

1.8 Limitations of ADR

Fails under severe power imbalance, lack of good faith, need for legal precedent, or non-arbitrable issues (criminal matters/public law violations).

Chapter 2

Conflict Styles & Negotiation Strategies

Mastering the Dual-Concern Model, positional vs. interest-based bargaining, and evaluating the psychological impacts of strategic postures.

2.3 The Dual-Concern Model

An individual's behavioral response to conflict is determined by two dimensions: Assertiveness (Concern for Self) and Cooperativeness (Concern for Others).

Assertiveness (Self) →
Cooperativeness (Other) →

Competing

High Self / Low Other

Aggressive anchors, threats. Optimal for emergencies or enforcing compliance. Risk: Relational damage.

Collaborating

High Self / High Other

Joint problem solving, logrolling, expanding the pie. Optimal for complex, high-value outcomes.

Avoiding

Low Self / Low Other

Withdrawal, postponement. Optimal for trivial issues or emotional cooling off. Risk: Unresolved conflict.

Accommodating

Low Self / High Other

Yielding to demands. Optimal when preserving goodwill outweighs current gains. Risk: Exploitation.

Compromising

Moderate Self & Other.
Splitting the difference. Risk: Sub-optimization.

2.4 Strategies & Frameworks

2.4.1 Bargaining Frameworks

Positional Bargaining: Rigid positions & incremental concessions. Results in zero-sum dynamics and strained relationships.

Interest-Based (Principled): Focuses on underlying needs/fears. Separates people from the problem. Uncovers integrative potential.

2.4.2 Core Structural Variables

  • Target Point (TP): Optimal goal / realistic best-case.
  • Reservation Price (RP): The walk-away point / absolute minimum/maximum.
  • BATNA: Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. Defines the RP and provides leverage.
  • ZOPA: Zone of Possible Agreement (Overlap between Buyer RP and Seller RP).

2.5 Negotiation Postures

Negotiators exhibit distinct behavioral postures that signal their intentions and risk tolerance:

Hard (Adversarial)

Focuses exclusively on claiming value. Treats opponent as adversary, relies on high anchors, pressure, and take-it-or-leave-it demands.

Soft (Relational)

Focuses on reaching agreement and harmony. Makes early/generous concessions. Highly vulnerable to value extraction.

Principled (Integrative)

Hard on the problem, soft on the people. Uses objective standards and explores options for mutual gain with firm boundaries.

Defensive (Reactive)

Adopted in response to hostility or power imbalance. Focuses on risk mitigation and info gathering before committing.

2.6 Numerical Case: SARL El-Bled Commercial Lease

3-year commercial lease negotiation between Tenant (SARL El-Bled) and Owner (EURL Immobilier).

Owner (Seller) Claims
  • Anchor: 1,200,000 / mo
  • Target (TPs): 1,000,000
  • Res. Price (RPs): 850,000
Tenant (Buyer) Claims
  • Anchor: 700,000 / mo
  • Target (TPb): 800,000
  • Res. Price (RPb): 950,000

2.6.2 ZOPA Calculation

ZOPA = [RPs, RPb]
ZOPA = [850k, 950k]
Surplus = 100,000 / month
3-Year Surplus = 3,600,000 DZD

Scenario A: Pure Distributive Bargaining (Split)

If parties negotiate purely on rent using a Compromising approach, they split the 100k surplus.

Settlement = (850k + 950k) / 2 = 900,000 DZD/month

Scenario B: Integrative Expansion (Logrolling)

Introduce non-monetary issues: Advance Payment (high value to Owner liquidity, low cost to Tenant reserves) and Maintenance (low cost to Tenant's in-house crew, high value to Owner avoiding contractors).

Trade-off Agreement: Rent set at 830,000 (Below Owner's RP!), 1-year advance, Tenant handles maintenance.
Owner Net Income: 830k + 25k (liq) + 29.1k (maint) = 884,167/mo (Exceeds Target!)
Tenant Net Expense: 830k + 8.3k (cash) + 12.5k (crew) = 850,833/mo (Below RP!)
Conclusion: Logrolling expanded the total negotiable value, making both parties better off than the distributive split.

Moving Toward a Positive Outcome: Distributive Negotiation

Mastering zero-sum tactical value-claiming, the anchoring effect, concession dynamics, and neutralizing aggressive hardball tactics.

3.2 The Structural Architecture of the Bargaining Zone

Distributive negotiation divides a fixed pool of resources (zero-sum/constant-sum game). The primary objective is to maximize one's own share of the surplus.

Seller RP

BATNA

Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. The benchmark providing leverage. Directly defines the Reservation Price.

Reservation Price (RP)

The walk-away quantitative threshold. Max price a buyer will pay; Min price a seller will accept.

Target Point (TP)

The aspirational goal or realistic optimal outcome a negotiator strives to achieve.

3.3 Value-Claiming Tactics

3.3.1 The Anchoring Effect

A cognitive bias where initial exposure serves as a reference point. First-Mover Advantage: Anchor aggressively if market knowledge is high. Defer if knowledge is low to avoid anchoring against yourself.

3.3.2 Concession Management

Concessions signal limits. Pacing is key (rapid signals weakness). The Decay Signal:

#1: DZD 100,000 → Large (Exploratory)
#2: DZD 50,000 → Moderate
#3: DZD 15,000 → Small
#4: DZD 5,000 → Final limit reached

Rule: Never concede without a counter-demand (Reciprocity).

3.3.3 Commitment Tactics

Explicit, unyielding stance. Requires finality, specificity, and clear consequences. Risk: Public deadlock if no face-saving escape exists.

3.4 Hardball Tactics & Defenses

High-pressure methods designed to force concessions. Must be identified and neutralized.

Good Cop / Bad Cop

Partner shifts between aggressive and reasonable.

Defense: Call out tactic explicitly; demand single rep.

Lowball / Highball

Extreme opening anchor to warp RP estimation.

Defense: Refuse to counter until withdrawn; re-anchor via objective standards.

The Nibble

Small extra request (e.g., free delivery) at the moment of closing.

Defense: Hold the line: "If I concede this, what will you give in return?"

Calculated Deadlines

Artificial time limits to force rushed decisions.

Defense: Test validity; offer a counter-schedule.

Chapter 4

Moving Toward a Positive Outcome: Integrative Negotiation

Shifting from claiming value to creating value, mastering the 4-step process, Pareto efficiency, and synergistic multi-issue utility trade-offs.

4.2 Distributive vs. Integrative Approach

Distributive (Fixed Pie)

50% | 50%
  • Focus: Positions ("What I want")
  • Resource: Fixed pie (Zero-sum)
  • Info Sharing: Concealed, misleading
  • Outcome: Compromise / Split

Integrative (Expanded Pie)

70 | 70
  • Focus: Interests ("Why I want it")
  • Resource: Expandable pie (Win-Win)
  • Info Sharing: Transparent, open
  • Outcome: Synergy, logrolling

4.3 The 4-Step Integrative Process

1

Identify & Define Problem

Define neutrally as a joint goal (e.g. "How can we structure the contract so speeds increase while costs stay in budget?"). Keep separate from solutions.

2

Uncover Underlying Interests

Discover Substantive (economic), Process, Relationship, and Principle interests driving public demands.

3

Generate Creative Options

Brainstorming, Logrolling (trading off varying priorities), Expanding the Pie, Bridging Solutions, Nonspecific Compensation.

4

Evaluate & Select via Criteria

Filter against independent, objective standards (market value, precedents). Use weighted matrices to optimize joint utility.

4.4 Pareto Efficiency

Value creation is formally measured using the Pareto Efficiency Criterion. The goal is to move from suboptimal interior points outward to the frontier.

Utility (B) →
Utility (A) →
A (Compromise)
B (Pareto Frontier)

Pareto Frontier (B): No party can increase utility without decreasing the utility of the other. Obstacle (4.6): The "Fixed-Pie Perception" keeps negotiators at Point A.
