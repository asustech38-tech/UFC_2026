Chapter 1

Purposes of the Master's Dissertation

Defining the Master's dissertation, identifying key features of Master's-level research, and recognizing the essential structural elements of academic writing.

1.1 / 1.2 What is a Master's Dissertation?

An important piece of academic research undertaken at the end of a Master's degree. It requires systematic investigation, critical thinking, and academic communication.

For English for Management students, it combines Research Competence (identifying problems, reviewing literature, analyzing data) and Academic English Competence (structuring arguments, integrating sources).

A dissertation normally involves:

  • Identifying a topic and formulating a problem.
  • Developing research questions and objectives.
  • Reviewing relevant academic literature.
  • Selecting methodology, collecting, and analyzing data.
  • Presenting findings, drawing conclusions, and recommending.

1.3 Purpose of a Master's Dissertation

Independent Research: Taking responsibility beyond textbooks.
Critical Thinking: Evaluating knowledge, identifying gaps.
Apply Theory: Using concepts on a specific management issue.
Contribute: Applying theories to new contexts or populations.

1.4 Characteristics of Master's-Level Research

  • Criticality: Do not just report; evaluate and compare perspectives.
  • Coherence: Logical connection (Problem → Questions → Methodology → Findings).
  • Evidence-based: Support claims with academic evidence, not personal opinion.
  • Academic English: Language must be formal, precise, objective, and cautious.

1.5 The Main Structure of a Dissertation

A Master's dissertation consists of interconnected sections. The exact requirements depend on the university template, but generally include:

1. Preliminary Sections

  • Title Page: Defined by department (Title, name, degree, date).
  • Declaration & Acknowledgements
  • Abstract: Brief, concise summary.
  • Keywords: Used to index the contribution.
  • TOC, Lists (Figures, Tables, Abbreviations)
  • Glossary

2. Core Chapters

  • Introduction: Context, problem, questions, objectives.
  • Literature Review: Theoretical framework and prior studies.
  • Methodology: Research design, data collection/analysis.
  • Results / Findings: Presenting the collected data.
  • Discussion: Interpreting findings against literature.
  • Conclusion: Summary, implications, limitations.

3. End Matter

  • Reference List: Proper citation of all academic sources used to avoid plagiarism.
  • Appendices (Optional): Questionnaires, interview transcripts, large tables, supplementary raw data.
Chapter 2

Selecting the Topic & Formulating the Problem

Transitioning from a broad research interest into a specifically defined, clear, and feasible research project within the Management field.

2.1 The 6-Step Research Formulation Process

1

Determine Interests

Identify recent motivations, prior knowledge, and align with academic/career goals.

2

Explore Literature

Review latest journals/reports. Note areas of debate, convergence, and divergence.

3

Identify Gaps

Find unanswered questions, contradictions, or unstudied populations/factors.

4

Narrow Down

Move from broad area to focused niche. Ensure viability, specificity, and manageable scope.

5

Formulate Q's

Develop main and subsidiary questions drawn directly from the identified problem.

6

Define Statement

Use a brief paragraph describing the problem, its implications, and what the study addresses.

Area → Topic → Problem → Gap → Questions → Objectives → Hypotheses

2.2 & 2.4 Narrowing the Research Area

A Master's dissertation cannot investigate an entire field (e.g., Management). It must move from a broad topic to a highly specific, manageable one.

Broad Area: Leadership
Specific Topic: Transformational Leadership
Adding Variables: ...and employee performance
Dissertation Topic: The Impact of Transformational Leadership on Employee Performance in Algerian SMEs.

Make it manageable by specifying: What is studied, Who is studied, Where, and When.

2.5 & 2.18 Evaluating Topic Feasibility

Before finalizing, ask these criteria questions to ensure the topic is realistic and valuable:

  • Is it relevant to the Management field?
  • Is it sufficiently narrowed down?
  • Is there enough academic literature / sources?
  • Is the research problem identifiable and clear?
  • Data & Access: Can you access the data and the target populations?
  • Time: Can it be done within the Master's schedule?
  • Skills: Do you possess the required methodological/analytical skills?
  • Ethics: Can it be conducted while protecting participants?

2.6 - 2.8 The Problem Statement & Research Gap

The topic tells us what area is being studied. The research problem explains what needs to be investigated and why.

Identifying a Research Gap (2.7)

A gap is something insufficiently understood or unresolved. It may result from:

  • Limited research on a particular population or geography.
  • Contradictory findings in existing literature.
  • An emerging organizational issue.

4 Stages of a Problem Statement (2.8)

1. Establish Context: Introduce general issue.
2. Present Knowledge: Explain what previous research established.
3. Identify Gap: Explain what remains unclear (the problem).
4. Need for Research: Explain why the proposed study is necessary.

2.9 - 2.11 Questions & Objectives

Principle (2.13): Research questions ask what the researcher wants to know; research objectives state what the researcher intends to do to answer those questions.

Research QuestionResearch Objective
What financial incentives are used by SMEs?To identify the financial incentives used by SMEs.
How do employees perceive financial incentives?To examine employees' perceptions of financial incentives.
How do incentives affect employee motivation?To investigate the effects of incentives on motivation.

2.19 Common Problems in Topic Formulation

  • Too Broad: "Management in Algeria" → Narrow it down.
  • Too Vague: "A Study of Employee Motivation" → Specify variables.
  • No Identifiable Problem: A topic alone isn't a problem; must explain what is unknown.
  • Too Many Variables: Linking leadership + motivation + culture + communication = Impossible for Master's.
  • Inaccessible Data: Do not select topics requiring unobtainable proprietary info.

2.10 Types of Research Questions

Descriptive

Describes a situation.
What practices are used?

Exploratory

Explores perceptions/processes.
How do employees perceive...?

Explanatory

Investigates causes/relationships.
How does X influence Y?

Comparative

Compares groups/contexts.
How does X differ between A & B?

Evaluative

Assesses effectiveness or impact. How effective are financial incentives?

2.14 / 2.15 Variables & Hypotheses

In quantitative research, define clear variables and testable predictions:

Independent Variable: Financial Incentives
→ Influences →
Dependent Variable: Employee Motivation

H1 (Alternative): Transformational leadership has a positive/significant effect on performance.
H0 (Null): Has NO significant effect.

2.20 Integrated Example Applying the Flow

Research Area
Human Resource Management
Research Topic
Employee motivation
Specific Topic
The Impact of Financial and Non-Financial Incentives on Employee Motivation in Algerian SMEs.
Research Problem (Gap)
There is limited understanding of the relative influence of financial and non-financial incentives on employee motivation in Algerian SMEs.
Main Question
"How do financial and non-financial incentives affect employee motivation in Algerian SMEs?"

2.21 Academic English Phrases

Develop appropriate language for formal academic research formulation:

Introducing Topic: "This study focuses on...", "The present research examines...", "This dissertation investigates..."
Identifying Gap: "Despite considerable research into X, limited attention has been paid to Y...", "A gap can be identified concerning..."
Justifying/Aim: "This issue is particularly important because...", "The aim of this study is to..."
Appropriate Verbs (2.12): identify, examine, investigate, analyze, evaluate, assess, explore, compare, determine.

Course Conclusion

"A dissertation is more than writing; it is a demonstration of academic independence, requiring a systematic transition from a broad area of interest down to a highly specific, answerable research problem."
