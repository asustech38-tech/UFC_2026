Chapter 1

Foundations of Research in Management

Understanding the fundamental role of research design, distinguishing between quantitative and qualitative methodologies, and framing testable hypotheses.

1.1 The Role of Research

Research is a systematic investigation to establish facts and draw conclusions. In the management context, research involves obtaining information and uncovering truths to improve managerial practice and decision-making.

1.1.2 Principles of Research Design

Cross-sectional study

Data collected from a population at a specific, single point in time.

Longitudinal study

Data collected repeatedly from the same sample over an extended period.

1.2.1 Quantitative vs. Qualitative

1.2.3 The Quantitative Research Process

1. Identify Problem

Define issue & review literature.

2. Formulate Q's

Develop hypotheses from questions.

3. Design Research

Select approach & sampling plan.

4. Collect Data

Deploy instruments & gather primary/secondary data.

5. Analyze Data

Clean data & apply statistics.

6. Report Findings

Interpret results & advise management.

1.2.4 Hypotheses Formulation

A hypothesis is a tentative proposition about the relationship between two or more variables, formulated for empirical testing.

Example Structure:

Research Question: Does employee training improve productivity?

Null Hypothesis (H₀): There is no significant difference in productivity between trained and untrained employees.
Alternative (H₁): Trained employees have significantly higher productivity than untrained employees.

1.2.5 Types of Variables & Scales

Independent Variable (IV)Manipulated or controlled to measure its impact.
Dependent Variable (DV)Expected to change in response to the IV; the outcome.
Mediating VariableTransmits the effect between IV and DV.
Confounding VariableExtraneous variable that correlates with both, distorting results.

Measurement Scales:

  • Nominal: Categories without order (e.g., Gender, Department).
  • Ordinal: Ordered categories, unequal intervals (e.g., Satisfaction rankings).
  • Interval: Ordered, equal intervals, no true zero (e.g., Temperature).
  • Ratio: Ordered, equal intervals, true zero (e.g., Revenue, Age).

1.3 Ethical Considerations in Research

Core Principles

  • Informed Consent: Voluntary agreement with full information.
  • Confidentiality: Protecting participant identities.
  • Avoidance of Harm: No physical/psychological damage.
  • Honesty: Accurate reporting without fabrication.

Common Ethical Violations

  • Data Manipulation: Selectively reporting results (p-hacking).
  • Sample Bias: Selecting samples that intentionally skew results.
  • Misuse of Data: Using data for uncommunicated purposes.
Chapter 2

Data Collection and Management

Designing appropriate sampling strategies, capturing data, and executing the critical data cleaning and coding phases required before analysis.

2.1 Primary vs Secondary Data

1. Primary Data

Collected specifically for the current research. Highly relevant but consumes time and money.

  • Observation: Structured (quantitative) or Participant (qualitative).
  • Interviews: Structured, semi-structured, or unstructured.
  • Questionnaires: Open and closed-ended prompts via mail, online, or SMS.

2. Secondary Data

Data previously collected by others for different purposes. Fast, but quality/relevance may vary.

Ex: Internal company records, Gov statistics, Academic DBs (Compustat).

2.2 Sampling Methods

Sampling is more practical, less expensive, and faster than conducting a full population Census.

Probability Sampling

Every element has a known, non-zero probability of selection. Allows statistical generalization.

  • Simple Random: Equal chance of selection (e.g., random employee survey).
  • Systematic: Selecting every kth element from a list.
  • Stratified: Population divided into strata; random sampling from each.
  • Cluster: Population divided into clusters; random selection of entire clusters.

Non-Probability Sampling

Probability of selection is unknown. Common in management when probability sampling is impractical.

  • Convenience: Selecting most accessible elements (Pilot studies).
  • Purposive/Judgmental: Based on researcher's specific expertise requirements.
  • Quota: Predetermined number from each subgroup based on demographics.
  • Snowball: Initial participants refer others (for hard-to-reach populations).

2.3 Data Management & Preparation Phases

Phase 1: Coding

Assign Numerical Codes (e.g., 1=Male, 2=Female). Create Value Labels in software.

Phase 2: Entry

Enter data into SPSS/Excel. Ensure Accuracy via Double-Entry or Validation Rules.

Phase 3: Cleaning

Check Errors. Handle Missing Data (Mean Sub/Imputation). Address Outliers (Winsorize).

Phase 4 & 5: Setup

Compute New Variables. Recode. Check Normality (Q-Q Plots). Assess Multicollinearity.

Activity 1: Simple Random Sample

Context: A manufacturing company has 9,000 employees. A researcher wants to study job satisfaction using a sample of 450.

Probability: 450 / 9,000 → 1 in 20. (Sampling fraction is n/N).

Execution Steps:

  1. Define Population (N = 9,000 full-time employees).
  2. Devise sampling frame (personnel records excluding part-timers).
  3. Assign consecutive numbers 1 to 9,000.
  4. Use a random number generator to select 450 unique numbers.

Handling Random Tables: If a table generates 5-digit numbers (e.g., 09188, 90045, 73189), take the last four digits (9188, 0045, 3189). Discard numbers exceeding 9000 (like 9188) and select the valid ones.

Activity 2: Interview Questions

Application: Formulate professional responses for managerial data collection scenarios.

Decision Making

"How do you assemble relevant data to make decisions? How do you know you have enough?"

Example Response: "I start by defining the core problem (Phase 1), then query internal ERP/CRM databases for structured historical data. I know I have enough data when the margin of error (E) in my sampling formula aligns with the required confidence level, and subsequent data points stop altering the descriptive trends significantly."

Leadership & Execution

"When you recommend something to management, what approach do you usually use?"

Example Response: "I use a quantitative, evidence-based approach. I present visual descriptive statistics first to establish context, outline my hypothesis testing results to prove statistical significance, and conclude with clear, actionable business implications rather than just raw numbers."

Course Conclusion
