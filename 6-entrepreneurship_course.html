A comprehensive guide to understanding the entrepreneurial journey—from ideation and opportunity recognition to the psychological traits of founders and the architecture of platform-based business models.

Chapter 1

Basics of Entrepreneurship

Exploring the core definitions, essential characteristics, economic importance, and the distinction between small businesses and innovation-driven enterprises.

1.1 Concept of Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is complex, chaotic, and non-linear. Different scholars highlight various facets of the process:

"...develop our student's discovery, reasoning and implementation skills so that they may excel in highly uncertain environments."

- Neck and Greene (2011)

"A dynamic and social process where individuals... identify opportunities for innovation and act upon these by transforming ideas into practical activities."

- Kollie et al. (2011)

"The dynamic process of creating incremental wealth by individuals who assume major risks in terms of equity, time, and career."

- Mokaya et al. (2013)

1.2 Characteristics of Entrepreneurship

A set of attitudes and behaviors that influence how entrepreneurs identify opportunities and respond to challenges (Solymossy, 1998):

AchievementMotivation to set goals and achieve high standards.
Personal ControlBelief that one can influence outcomes directly.
InnovationWillingness to implement new methods/products.
Self-EsteemConfidence in one's entrepreneurial abilities.
OpportunismTendency to recognize and seize profitable gaps.
Risk ToleranceModerate risk-takers willing to manage uncertainty.
Autonomy/IndependenceAbility to act and decide without relying on others' approval.

1.4 Types of Entrepreneurship

Innovation-Driven (IDE)

Pursues global opportunities through new-to-the-world innovations offering clear competitive advantage and high growth potential (Aulet & Murray, 2013).

  • Targets global or broad regional markets.
  • Requires external capital (Venture Capital, Angels).
  • Involves significant technical and market risks.
  • Generates substantial direct/indirect employment.

Small Business (SME)

Serves primarily local markets through traditional, well-understood business ideas with limited competitive advantage.

  • Targets local demand.
  • Relies on founder's execution and business acumen.
  • Important source of self and local employment.
  • Small scale (founder, family, limited workers).
Based on Motivation (Stam & van Stel, 2009):
Necessity-Driven
Undertaken because individuals have no other alternatives for earning a living. Prevalent in low-income countries.
Opportunity-Driven
Motivated by the desire to pursue opportunities, independence, or higher income. Prevalent in high-income countries.
Growth-Oriented
Strong focus on business expansion. Crucial for economic growth in transition economies.
Chapter 2

The Entrepreneur: Concept & Characteristics

Focusing on the central actor in the entrepreneurial process. Who they are, how they are defined, and the specific psychological competencies that drive success.

2.1 Who is an Entrepreneur?

No single definition is universally accepted. The concept has evolved:

  • Jean Baptiste Say (c. 1800) A person who transfers resources from lower productivity to higher productivity and increased profit, thus creating value. (Resource coordinator perspective).
  • Bygrave and Hofer (1991) A person who perceives an opportunity and creates an organization to follow it. (Opportunity recognition perspective).
  • Agarwal & Chatterjee (2007) An individual who employs his own skills to shape a new organization out of complexity and uncertainty. (Skill and uncertainty perspective).

2.2.1 The "3C" Characteristics

Successful entrepreneurs are characterized by three complementary psychological traits that enhance venture performance (Raine & Pandya, 2019):

Curiosity

Optimism and openness that enhance company growth and discovery.

Creativity

Develops innovative solutions and facilitates conflict management.

Commitment

Passion and perseverance strongly associated with higher venture performance.

2.2.2 Personal Entrepreneurial Competencies (PECs)

A specific set of knowledge, skills, and abilities. The Harvard University framework identifies three major clusters of competencies (Arafeh, 2016):

1. Achievement Cluster

  • Opportunity-seeking and initiative.
  • Persistence in the face of obstacles.
  • Fulfillment of commitments.
  • Demand for quality and efficiency.
  • Taking calculated risks.

2. Planning Cluster

  • Goal-setting (clear, long-term visions).
  • Information-seeking (market research, feedback).
  • Systematic planning and monitoring.

3. Power Cluster

  • Persuasion and networking.
  • Independence.
  • Self-confidence.
Chapter 3

Entrepreneurial Opportunity

The structured journey from initial business idea generation to formal opportunity evaluation, the business case, and the final pitch deck.

3.1.2 Opportunity Recognition & Development

According to Ardichvili et al. (2003), entrepreneurs go through three main stages to transform an idea into a viable business:

1. Recognition

Perceiving market needs and creating a "fit" with specific resources.

2. Development

Proactive, continuous efforts to build an entire business concept, not just a product.

3. Evaluation

Assessing feasibility, risks, and potential to decide whether to continue or abandon.

3.1.1 Idea Generation

The systematic process of creating and capturing ideas. Entrepreneurs use creative techniques to respond to emerging market gaps:

Brainstorming SCAMPER Empathy Map Design Thinking Focus Groups Mind Mapping 5W's & H

3.2.1 The Business Case

A structured assessment of an opportunity determining if it is worth pursuing. It provides the basis for a detailed Business Plan.

Three Essential Tasks (Volker, 2012):

  • Customers: Identifying who will buy and why.
  • Resources: Identifying capital required to achieve sales and be viable.
  • Financial Returns: Estimating returns and determining money generated over time.

3.2.2 The Pitch Deck

A brief presentation providing an overview of a startup idea, delivered to investors. It serves as a visual aid to communicate value proposition and growth potential.

Modes of Expression:

  • A short written executive summary.
  • An oral presentation in face-to-face meetings ("Elevator Pitch").
  • A stage presentation with an audience acting as a jury.
  • A short video or prototype app demo.
Chapter 4

Business Model Generation

Understanding the logic of value creation through the Business Model Canvas and the transition toward modern platform-based architectures.

4.1.1 Concept of a Business Model

A business model is a simplified representation of how a business operates in the real world, illustrating how its key elements interact to create, deliver, and capture value.

Teece (2010) "Articulates the logic and provides data that demonstrates how a business creates value, outlining the architecture of revenues, costs, and profits."
Porter (via Morris, 2005) "Builds upon the value chain concept and extended notions of value systems and strategic positioning."
Amit & Zott (2012) "An activity system conducted to satisfy market needs, specifying which parties conduct which activities and how they link."

4.1.2 The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder & Pigneur)

Key Partnerships
The network of suppliers and partners that make the business model work (e.g., Foxconn for Apple).
Key Activities
The most important actions a company must take to operate successfully.
Key Resources
Important assets (physical, financial, human, IP) required to offer value.
Value Proposition
The bundle of products and services that solve a problem or create quantitative/qualitative value for a specific segment.
Cust. Relationships
Types of relationships established (personal assistance, self-service, automated).
Channels
How the company communicates with and reaches segments to deliver value.
Customer Segments
The different groups of people or organizations the enterprise aims to reach and serve.
Cost Structure
All costs incurred to operate the business model (fixed, variable, R&D, marketing).
Revenue Streams
The cash generated from each segment (asset sale, subscription, licensing, advertising).

4.2 Platform-Based Models

An infrastructure that facilitates interactions and trade among two or more different types of economic actors, using digital technologies to reduce friction (Croxson et al., 2021).

FactorTraditionalPlatform-Based
ValueProducing & selling goods.Facilitating interactions.
ScaleRequires physical assets.Grows via Network Effects.
DataImproves internal processes.Facilitates better matching.
RisksSupply chain, inventory.Data integrity, platform trust.

4.3 Successful Examples

Apple Value Prop: Premium design, iOS ecosystem. Revenue: Hardware sales, App Store commissions.
Amazon Value Prop: Vast selection, fast delivery. Key Resource: Logistics network, AWS cloud infrastructure.
Uber Value Prop: On-demand transport. Key Partners: Independent drivers. Network Effects scale the platform.
Airbnb Value Prop: Authentic travel experiences. Key Activities: Matching guests/hosts, maintaining trust and platform UI.

Course Conclusion
