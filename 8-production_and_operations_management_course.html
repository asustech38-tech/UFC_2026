Introduction to Production & Operations Management

Understanding the fundamental distinction between production and operations, identifying core management functions, and exploring production system typologies.

1.2 & 1.3 What is Production vs. Operations?

Production

The process of transforming inputs (raw materials, labor, machinery) into finished physical goods that meet customer needs and create added value. Strongly associated with manufacturing.

A broader concept referring to all activities involved in producing both goods and services. Because every organization performs activities that create value, every organization has an operations function.

1.5 Main Functions of Operations Management

1. Planning

Deciding what, how much, and when to produce. The primary management function.

2. Process Design

Developing efficient production and service workflows and selecting technology.

3. Capacity Planning

Ensuring sufficient resources to meet demand without unused cost overhead.

4. Inventory Mgmt

Controlling materials to avoid shortages and excess stock storage costs.

5. Quality Mgmt

Ensuring products/services meet standards (planning, assurance, control).

6. SCM & Logistics

Managing the flow of materials, info, and products from supplier to customer.

7. Maintenance

Keeping equipment and facilities in safe, working condition to prevent delays.

8. Continuous Imprv.

Ongoing efforts to eliminate waste via Lean Production, Kaizen, and Six Sigma.

1.6 Types of Production Systems

  • Job ProductionManufacturing one or a few customized products. High flexibility, skilled labor, but higher costs and longer times.
  • Batch ProductionProducing in groups. Equipment is adjusted between batches. Balances flexibility and efficiency for moderate quantities.
  • Mass ProductionLarge-scale standardized production using assembly lines. High volume, low variation, low unit cost, repetitive.
  • Continuous ProductionProcess operates 24/7 with minimal interruption. Highly automated, high capital intensity (e.g., chemicals, petroleum).

1.9 Practical Activity: The Local Bakery

Context: A local bakery purchases flour, sugar, butter, eggs, and yeast. These ingredients are mixed, baked, packaged, and sold.

1. Identify the inputs: Flour, sugar, butter, eggs, yeast, labor, baking equipment, electricity, and recipes.
2. Describe the transformation process: Ingredients are mixed, baked, cooled, packaged, and prepared for sale.
3. Identify the outputs: Bread, cakes, and pastries.
4. Is this a manufacturing or service operation? Manufacturing operation, because tangible physical products are produced from raw materials.
Note (1.8 Trends): Today, the bakery might also use Digital Transformation (e.g., IoT ovens, delivery apps) and Sustainability (reducing food waste).

Operations Strategy and Competitiveness

Aligning operational resources with organizational goals, mastering the five competitive priorities, and leveraging operations for sustainable advantage.

2.2 & 2.3 Operations Strategy & Alignment

Operations Strategy is the long-term pattern of decisions determining how an organization's operations support its overall business strategy. Competitiveness is the ability to create and deliver superior value compared to competitors.

Business StrategyOperations Strategy
Achieves organizational goals & competitive positioning.Develops operational capabilities to support those goals.
Determines what the organization intends to achieve.Determines how operations will achieve those objectives.
Developed by top management.Implemented by operations managers collaboratively.

2.4 Competitive Priorities

The operational capabilities organizations develop to create value and achieve advantage. Successful firms integrate multiple priorities simultaneously.

Cost

Producing efficiently to minimize expenses (lean, standardization).

Quality

Meeting/exceeding expectations (reliability, durability, low defects).

Delivery Speed

Providing products rapidly and reliably (short lead times).

Flexibility

Responding quickly to volume, variety, or tech changes.

Innovation

Developing new products and creative operational practices to differentiate.

2.5 & 2.6 Creating Advantage & Organizational Performance

  • Cost Advantage: Achieved through economies of scale, process automation, and effective SCM. Allows firms to attract price-sensitive customers and improve market share.
  • Quality Advantage: Reduces warranty claims and inefficiencies while increasing customer trust, reputation, and loyalty.
  • Flexibility Advantage: Allows rapid introduction of new products, customization, and capacity adjustment with minimal disruption in dynamic environments.
  • Speed Advantage: Capitalizes on market opportunities faster. Crucial in e-commerce and digital supply chains.
2.6 Impact: An effective operations strategy directly optimizes resource utilization, reduces costs, and enhances productivity, leading to improved financial performance, resilience, and sustainable success.

Chapter 3

Process Design and Analysis

Systematic planning of workflows, classifying process types by volume and variety, mathematically evaluating capacity, and applying Lean Six Sigma.

3.2 The Role of Process Design

Effective process design enables organizations to improve operational performance across 10 key dimensions:

  • Improves Efficiency: Eliminates non-value-added activities.
  • Enhances Quality: Standardizes control to minimize defects.
  • Supports Strategy: Aligns workflows with competitive priorities.
  • Increases Productivity: Optimizes resource allocation.
  • Optimizes Utilization: Reduces idle capacity of labor/equipment.
  • Reduces Costs: Lowers inventory and defect rates.
  • Improves Flexibility: Enhances organizational resilience.
  • Facilitates Continuous Improvement: Foundation for Lean/Kaizen.
  • Enhances Customer Satisfaction: Improves delivery and reliability.
  • Supports Digital Transformation: Integrates AI and automation.

3.4 & 3.7 Process Analysis & Lean Six Sigma

Analysis uses tools like flowcharts and Value Stream Mapping to identify bottlenecks. Improvement frameworks include:

Lean Management

Focuses on eliminating waste (overproduction, waiting, transport, defects) to maximize flow. Tools: 5S, JIT.

Six Sigma

Data-driven framework (DMAIC) to reduce process variation and defects.

Kaizen

Continuous incremental improvements involving all employees.

BPR

Business Process Reengineering: Fundamental, radical redesign of processes.

Bottleneck (3.6): A stage where workflow is restricted because its capacity is lower than demand, limiting overall system throughput.

3.5 - 3.10 Mathematics of Capacity

Definitions: Design Capacity is theoretical maximum. Effective Capacity accounts for planned constraints (maintenance/breaks).

Utilization (%) = (Actual Output / Design Capacity) × 100
Efficiency (%) = (Actual Output / Effective Capacity) × 100

Activity: Factory Calculation

Design Capacity = 1,000 units/day
Effective Capacity = 900 units/day
Actual Output = 810 units/day

Utilization = (810 / 1000) × 100 = 81%
Efficiency = (810 / 900) × 100 = 90%

Interpretation: The factory utilizes 81% of its maximum design capacity and operates at 90% efficiency relative to its effective capacity. This indicates good operational performance while leaving some unused capacity for future demand.

Chapter 4

Facility Location and Layout

Evaluating geographical location factors and selecting physical floorplan layouts to optimize material handling, workflow, and productivity.

4.2 & 4.3 Types of Facility Layouts: Advantages & Limitations

Product (Line) Layout

Arranges workstations by production sequence. Ideal for mass production.

High productivity, low handling cost. Low flexibility, line stoppages.

Process (Functional)

Groups similar machines by function. Products take varied routes. High customization.

High flexibility, customized output. High handling cost, complex routing.

Fixed-Position

Product stays stationary; workers/tools move to it (e.g., ships, planes).

Minimal product movement. High labor/transport cost, hard to coordinate.

Hybrid (Cellular)

Combines product and process. Dedicated work cells for product families.

Balances efficiency & flexibility. Higher design/implementation cost.
